Market Demand and Elasticity

The law of demand states that, holding other factors constant, quantity demanded falls when a product's own price rises. A change in income, preferences, expectations, population, or the price of a related good shifts the demand curve instead of causing movement along it.

Price elasticity of demand measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. Demand is elastic when the percentage change in quantity demanded is larger than the percentage change in price. Demand tends to be less elastic when close substitutes are unavailable or buyers have little time to adjust.

A binding price ceiling is set below the market equilibrium price. At that controlled price, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, producing a shortage. Non-price allocation mechanisms such as waiting lines may then appear.

Supply and Market Adjustment

The law of supply states that producers generally offer a larger quantity when the market price rises, holding other influences constant. Input prices, production technology, expectations, the number of sellers, and taxes can shift supply. A movement along a supply curve instead results from a change in the product's own price.

Markets move toward equilibrium through incentives. When the current price is above equilibrium, excess supply puts downward pressure on price. When the current price is below equilibrium, excess demand puts upward pressure on price. This adjustment story does not imply that every market reaches equilibrium instantly or without transaction costs.

Consumer and Producer Surplus

Consumer surplus is the difference between a buyer's willingness to pay and the price actually paid. Producer surplus is the difference between the price a seller receives and the minimum amount the seller would accept. In the basic competitive model, gains from trade are maximized at the equilibrium quantity when no external costs or benefits are present.

An excise tax creates a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers receive. The traded quantity falls, some mutually beneficial trades no longer occur, and deadweight loss appears. The statutory assignment of the tax does not determine its economic incidence.

Externalities and Public Policy

A negative externality exists when an activity imposes costs on people who are not part of the market transaction. Because private decision makers do not bear the complete social cost, an unregulated market may produce more than the socially efficient quantity. A corrective tax can align private incentives more closely with social costs, although estimating the external harm can be difficult.
